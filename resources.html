01As someone with ADHD, this is what helped me The study methods that worked for me

I have ADHD, and throughout college I had to discover study techniques that helped me stay focused, understand concepts, and retain information. These are the methods that personally helped me.

  • Pomodoro Method

    I used focused study sessions with breaks in between instead of trying to study continuously for hours.

  • Active Recall

    I would study a topic, put my notes away, and try to remember what I had learned without looking at the answers. This helped me identify which concepts I understood and which ones needed more attention.

  • Teaching Myself the Lesson Back

    After learning something, I would explain it back to myself in my own words, almost as though I were teaching someone else. This helped me understand concepts rather than simply memorize them.

  • Creating My Own Quizzes

    I would create practice quizzes based on what I was studying. Quizzing myself helped me test my understanding, remember information, and identify weak areas.

  • Short Study Sessions

    I found that studying in shorter sessions worked better for me than forcing myself to sit for long periods. I focused on manageable amounts of information at a time.

02How I prepared for certifications My step-by-step process

I hold over 12 industry certifications, so I feel like I’ve got cert prep down (until they change the exams, lol). When it came to certification, this is what I did.

  1. Search Reddit and DiscordI search Reddit and Discord for tips and to gather the study notes and resources people used when they passed. I’m currently in college, so these communities have been a big help.
  2. Download the official exam objectivesThe objectives tell you exactly what can be on the exam. Everything on them is up for grabs.
  3. Turn the objectives into a study planI put the objectives into an AI tool like ChatGPT or Claude to create a study plan and clear up any gray areas. While I study, I have it break information down so simply that a toddler could understand it. You’re taking in a lot, so make it digestible for you.
  4. Pair my notes with videosOnce I have my study plan and notes, I pair them with YouTube videos. For Security+, I used Professor Messer’s videos, and he has study materials too.
  5. Prefer not to use AI? Use reputable study materialIf you’d rather not use ChatGPT, Claude, or other AI tools, look at who offers trusted study material. I’ve used Jason Dion and Professor Messer, and there are plenty of others. Do your research.
  6. Don’t overcomplicate itSee what’s being covered, study, take practice tests, and give it a shot.

If you don’t pass

It isn’t the end-all be-all. I’ll let you know now: I have failed certifications multiple times, and guess what? I’m still in the industry. Go back to the drawing board, make some adjustments, and execute.

You got this. If it was easy, everyone would be doing it.

03Build a portfolio you can speak to It doesn’t have to be a thousand labs

My Azure honeypot lab is the one hiring managers kept asking about. What made the difference wasn’t how many labs I had. It was being able to explain that one, step by step.

  1. Quality over quantityYou don’t need a thousand labs. Two to four labs you can explain front to back will do more for you than a long list you can’t talk about.
  2. Align it with what you’re applying toMake sure your labs match the roles you want. Read the job description, then build or highlight the labs that fit: a SOC role, show detection and investigation. An IAM role, show identity lifecycle and SSO. Cloud security, show cloud monitoring. GRC, show a risk assessment. If your labs don’t line up with the jobs you’re applying to, that’s your next lab.
  3. Know it front to backBe ready to explain what you built, why you built it, the choices you made, what went wrong, and what you’d do differently. The first time an interviewer asked me to walk through my lab, I wasn’t ready. After that, I made sure I knew every step.
  4. Practice “walk me through it” out loudHave a two-minute version of each lab: the problem, what you built, what you found, and what you learned. Say it out loud until it feels natural.
  5. Document as you goTake screenshots and notes while you work. It’s much harder to recreate them later, and they become your evidence.
  6. Write it up so others can followFor each lab, write the problem, the tools, the steps, the results, and your takeaways. Put it on GitHub or your own site so you can share one link.
  7. Keep it currentAdd new labs as you grow and update the old ones. Your portfolio grows with you.

Depth beats a long list.

04My resume process The steps behind the resume that landed my job

I’ve been getting a lot of questions about the resume that landed me my cybersecurity job, so here’s what I did.

Take this with a grain of salt

I already had tech experience, so this is what worked for me. Every job and background is different. Use what helps and leave what doesn’t.

  1. Start with a base resumePut the experience on there that fits the field. You’d be surprised how much of your current job you can tie into security. If you don’t have quote-unquote hands-on, production experience in the field, add labs. I had three labs max, and everyone kept asking about my honeypot lab, so I learned it front to back. The first time I interviewed, I didn’t.
  2. Grab the job descriptionMake sure the labs on your resume line up with that job.
  3. Put both into AI with your promptI use Claude (I used to use ChatGPT). Ask it to use the keywords from the job description and align your resume to the role. It took me a few tries to get it doing exactly what I wanted.
  4. Review everything, then adjust it yourselfAI will embellish. Tell it to align and reword, but not to embellish, and if it does, have it fix it. Then go through and adjust the wording yourself. A lot of people are using AI for their resumes, so many sound the same. Resumes that read as AI-written or stuffed with keywords can get filtered out by ATS or passed over by recruiters. Make sure it sounds like you.
  5. If you can’t speak to it, don’t put it on thereEverything on your resume is up for grabs in the interview. If there’s something you can’t talk about, go do the lab and learn it, or take it off.

The prompt I use

Tailor my resume to the job description below.

- Match my resume to the job's keywords, using the job's wording wherever it honestly describes my experience.
- Reorder my skills and bullets so the most relevant come first.
- Only include the certifications and projects that are relevant to this job.
- Only use what's already in my resume. Never add or exaggerate experience, tools, skills, or numbers.
- If the job asks for something that might be in my background but isn't clear from my resume, ask me before adding it.
- Keep my job titles, employers, and dates the same.
- Write it in a natural, human voice with ATS-friendly formatting (no tables or columns).
- After the resume, list any job requirements I don't meet so I can prepare for them.

MY RESUME:
[paste here]

JOB DESCRIPTION:
[paste here]

Before pasting your resume into any AI tool, remove your phone number, address, and email.

05College survival tips Lessons from finishing college with ADHD

Lessons from completing college while managing ADHD and other responsibilities.

  • Finding study methods that fit how you learn.
  • Breaking assignments and difficult topics into smaller pieces.
  • Using short study sessions to avoid feeling overwhelmed.
  • Taking breaks when needed.
  • Testing your knowledge instead of only rereading material.
  • Explaining concepts out loud to improve understanding.
  • Finding supportive learning communities.
  • Staying consistent without expecting perfection.
  • Remembering that everyone’s learning journey is different.
